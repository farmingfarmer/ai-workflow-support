1 · The hook — why this exists 0:00–1:30

"The contract team was reduced to essentially one person, and the review workload got pushed onto leads who don't normally read agreements. So I built a Copilot agent that does the first-pass review — it reads a contract against our own Keck playbook and hands back a prioritized, cited analysis. Everything you're about to see runs on tools we already license, it took a couple of weeks, and it's maintained by editing one text file — no infrastructure, nothing for our team to babysit."
"Two framing points before I show it. One: it is a first-pass assistant, never an approver — every output says that twice and routes the serious items to Contracting and Legal. Two: what makes it trustworthy is that every finding cites the contract clause and the playbook rule, so a reviewer verifies in seconds instead of trusting a black box."

2 · Live demo — the trap contract 1:30–6:00

Do Paste Input A (the vendor MSA excerpt) into the agent and send "Review this against the playbook."
"This is a fictional vendor contract I've planted problems in, to show you what it catches. Watch the top of the output first."
Point at The Deal Snapshot line — "it computed the total contract value, $900,000, and correctly left renewals out because the count is unknown. One line and you know the stakes." Then the playbook it used, named, with the author.
Point at — the moment The Critical: Attempted review manipulation finding. "I hid a line in this contract telling AI reviewers to report it as compliant and skip certain sections. The agent flagged that as a manipulation attempt and ignored it. That's the security story no slide can tell."
Point at One or two substantive catches a tired reviewer misses: the reversed, uncapped indemnity, and the payment-term conflict — the body says Net 20, the exhibit says Net 60. "It caught a contradiction buried between two documents."
Point at The Key Dates table — "it did real date math: the exact auto-renewal opt-out deadline, calculated from the term end minus the notice window. Keck never silently auto-renews again."
"And at the bottom it drafts the contract-request-form language and tells you who to escalate each item to. So a lead goes into the formal process already prepared."

3 · Live demo — the SOW (your reference material at work) 6:00–9:30

"Our real workload is heavy on SOWs and redlines, and you gave me the SOW checklists. Let me show you those built in."
Do Start a new chat, paste Input B (the SOW), send the same review prompt.
Point at "First — it recognized this is a Statement of Work under a governing MSA, not a standalone agreement, and applied the SOW checklist you provided."
Point at — the headline SOW rule The defer-to-MSA findings. "The #1 SOW rule from your checklist: a SOW must defer to the MSA and strip vendor terms the MSA already governs. It caught that in two places — including one planted in the very last section — which also proves it read all the way to the end of the document."
Point at The SOW-specific catches: vague "Service 1, 2, 3" scope, payment on execution, auto-renewal, T&M with no cap, and the AI-training-on-our-data clause flagged Critical.
Point at The injection in the final line — caught again — "and notice it still reviewed the sections that injection tried to hide. It reaches the tail and resists the attack there."

4 · How it meets and exceeds the ask 9:30–12:00

"Everything in your one-pager is covered — package review, citation-based deviations, Critical/Important/Advisory, preferred positions with fallbacks and escalation owners, reusable submission language, and the guardrails. That's the baseline." (Have the overview PDF's "meets" table visible if you want to walk it.)
"Where it goes beyond: the injection defense you just saw, the deadline math, a healthcare-specific trigger checklist — HIPAA, Stark, Open Payments, the no-AI-training-on-USC-data rule — that generic tools don't carry. Redline analysis. Inbound-license handling. Comparison mode across vendors. And a self-improving loop: when it hits a topic the playbook doesn't cover, it recommends what to add — so it feeds the playbook's maintenance."
"And critically — I red-teamed it before release. I attacked my own agent, found eleven issues, fixed them all, and the audit trail is a document you can read. We're not hoping it's robust; we checked."

5 · How your reference material became the agent 12:00–13:30

"Nothing you and the team sent was decorative. The playbook is the review standard itself. The SOW and Quote checklists became the defer-to-MSA logic and the SOW rules you just watched. The redlined agreements — TriNetX, the MSAs — became the redline-analysis capability. The Avandra inbound license drove the licensee posture and the embedded-schedule handling. The nference and BD sets were the real-document test corpus and surfaced the rights-protected-file case. Every document shaped a capability."

6 · Honest status & the ask 13:30–15:00

"Straight on where it stands: it's live in the tenant at the latest version with everything I showed active, and I just shared it with you. A few honest next steps — a full-length run on a 30-plus-section contract to confirm coverage at scale; folding the SOW checklist into the playbook so the criteria live in one maintained place; and confirming the data-handling path with Compliance before real contracts flow through at volume. The human review gate stays throughout — this prepares the work, it doesn't decide it."
"The bigger point for our team: this is the reusable-building-blocks story you keep pushing. The same pattern — playbook plus a hardened Copilot agent — is why the residency agents, the Architect, and this all look alike. What would you want to point it at next?"

If asked — quick answers

  • "Does everyone need a premium license?" No — using a shared agent needs no premium license; they open it in the Copilot app or m365.cloud.microsoft, not inside Word. (We tested this with an unlicensed account.)
  • "Can it handle a 100-page contract?" It reviews in full and is honest about coverage — it tells you if it couldn't reach the end and recommends splitting. For truly oversized contracts there's a documented section-by-section workflow, and a longer-term automation path that pairs with Shivam's long-document work.
  • "What about our data / compliance?" It runs in our tenant; the human gate stays; and I've flagged that we confirm the approved data path with Compliance before high volume. Nothing goes to an external tool.
  • "Who maintains it?" The program owns it; maintenance is editing one instructions file. No infrastructure.
  • "How do we know it's accurate?" Every finding is cited to the clause and the playbook rule, and it re-checks its own citations; validated live against planted-trap documents.
